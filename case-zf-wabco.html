01

The problem

Situation

Drivers of 40-tonne trucks glanced at cluttered tablets and separate radar beepers. Eyes-off-road time often went past 2.5 seconds, above EU driver-distraction guidance.

Night driving made it worse: bright screens hurt night vision and small touch targets were impossible at 90 km/h.

02

My role and how I led

What I did myself, and what I trusted the team with

What I owned

  • The three-zone layout and glance-time budget
  • Alignment with safety and homologation reviewers
  • Scaling the shared design system across squads

What I delegated

Process

From research to measured outcome

01

Research

Ride-alongs on night freight routes. Mapped every glance and every alarm.

02

Define

Set a glance budget: critical info under 0.8s, navigation under 1.2s.

03

Wireframe

Three zones: safety and speed, turn guidance, peripheral eco-coaching.

04

Prototype

Simulator builds with day and anti-glare night modes.

05

Test

Glance-time and heatmap tests in simulated rain, fog and tunnels.

06

Measure

Route errors and driver feedback tracked across the fleet rollout.

04

Design Deliverables

Core workflows, interface architecture, and shipped designs

05

Key decisions

The calls I made and what they cost

1

Three glance zones with strict priority

Why: Safety information must win every time something competes for attention.

Trade-off: Eco-coaching, a sales feature, was demoted to a peripheral light.

2

No touch interaction while driving

Why: Touch at 90 km/h on a vibrating cab caused repeated mis-taps.

Trade-off: Some settings moved to the parked state or the fleet app.

3

Dedicated anti-glare night palette

Why: Drivers dimmed screens to zero at night and missed alerts.

Trade-off: Two full colour modes to design, test and certify.

06

Results

Business and user impact

Average glance time dropped from 2.8 seconds to 0.94, well inside the 1.2-second guideline. Critical route errors fell 18% across 80,000+ trucks.

07

What I learned

In safety-critical UI, what you remove matters more than what you add. The hardest conversations were about what to leave out.
